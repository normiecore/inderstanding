# Idea bank

This is a set of directions for Inderstanding beyond the six prototypes already in progress (Prism, Vantage, Council, The Long Way, The Question Deck and Working Creed). Most of these ideas are not screens. They use paper, voice, objects, calendars, other people, walking and acts in the world, because those are the places where understanding forms, and software can at most set up the conditions for it. Each idea has a short description and one line on how it keeps the work with the person. The top five are explained first and marked in the list. Many ideas could sit together under one product as optional practices, and several would feed the existing prototypes. Several also need a server or a postal service (Night Line, Letters Across Time, matching for Chavruta and pen pals), which conflicts with the no-accounts rule in [principles.md](principles.md); those would need their own privacy design before going further.

## Top 5

1. **Clearness Committee (17).** It is a real, tested Quaker practice built around exactly the constraint the premise needs: other people help, and nobody is allowed to interpret for you. It costs nothing, the software's part is small and obvious, and it brings the user's friends into the process, which a solo app never does.
2. **Open Doors (26).** It answers the brief's own example (Christianity seen from another angle, or Daoism) by moving the comparison out of reading and into rooms with practitioners, which is where a tradition either strikes a chord or doesn't. It pairs well with Prism: lenses on screen, then the real thing on a Sunday or a Tuesday evening.
3. **Your Own Liturgical Year (9).** Traditions give their members a calendar that brings the same events round every year; this gives the user one built from their own life. Meaning builds up each time a date returns, it needs years to mature, and it can't be rushed.
4. **Ethical Will (37).** One letter a year, to people you love, that has to state plainly what you've come to believe about how to live. It combines time, other people and contribution in one practice, and the yearly versions become the clearest record of how you've changed.
5. **The Raft (13).** It deals with the dependence risk in the structure of the product and states the premise in product form: a tool for understanding that plans to become unnecessary. It would also set Inderstanding apart from every engagement-driven app the user has used.

## Form factors

### 1. Touchstone
A smooth pocket stone or wooden token with a pressure sensor and nothing else: no screen, light or sound. You squeeze it when something lands (a sting, a moment of ease, a flash of recognition) and it records only the time. Once a week the app lays the timestamps over your calendar and asks what was happening at each one, and you write about it or leave it. The presses you can't account for are worth noticing too.

**Why it fits:** it records that something mattered and leaves the question of what it was entirely to you.

### 2. Night Line
A phone number you can call from any phone. It plays one question in a human voice and records whatever you say for up to five minutes, and you never see a transcript. Twelve months later, on the same date, the recording is returned to you as audio with the question played first. Talking at night to no one is less edited than writing, and your own voice from a year ago is harder to argue with than a summary.

**Why it fits:** the only interpreter of the recording is the person who made it, a year later.

### 3. The Bell
A plain wristband or ring that gives one soft pulse three or four times a day at random. It borrows from the communities of Thich Nhat Hanh at Plum Village, where people stop at the sound of a bell and take three breaths, and from the experience sampling method psychologists use to catch ordinary moments. At the pulse you stop, notice what you are doing, and ask whether it sits with what you said matters. Nothing is logged unless you tap once.

**Why it fits:** alignment gets checked inside ordinary hours by your own attention, instead of being reconstructed later.

### 4. Commonplace Book
A well-made blank book with an indexing scheme printed in the back, adapted from the method John Locke published for keeping commonplace books. You copy by hand any passage from any tradition that catches you, then write a sentence of your own beside it about where it met your life. The index grows under headings you invent. The app's only role is an occasional nudge and an optional photo backup.

**Why it fits:** copying by hand is slow enough that you have to decide whether a line is worth it, and the headings are yours.

### 5. Almanac Frame
A small e-ink frame for a shelf or kitchen wall that changes once a day and has no buttons. It shows the season (by the 24 Chinese solar terms, the church year, or a calendar you set) and one line of your own words written on this date in an earlier year, if there is one. In the first year it is mostly blank.

**Why it fits:** it shows only material you produced, and it becomes useful only as you live more.

### 6. The Shelf
One shelf or corner of the home set aside for a few objects that stand for things in your life: a parent's watch, a stone from a walk, a letter. Many traditions keep a household shrine; this one makes no religious claim and holds only your things. Once a season you take one object away, add one, and write a few lines on why. Over the years the seasonal photos of the shelf become a record you can read without captions.

**Why it fits:** deciding what earns a place in the room is a judgement about what matters that nobody else can make.

### 7. Carried Question
A single card in your wallet with one question written by someone who knows you, in their handwriting. You can't write it yourself and the app can't generate it; it can only be replaced when another person gives you a new one. People might get one at a clearness committee (17) or from a friend over dinner.

**Why it fits:** the question comes from outside your own head, while the work of answering it stays with you.

### 8. The Offline Edition
The whole of Inderstanding as a box with no software at all: a deck, a commonplace book, a printed calendar, stamped envelopes, a credential for walking, and a guide to running a clearness committee. It is a product in its own right and also a test of the rest of the idea bank.

**Why it fits:** if a practice only works with an app, the app is probably doing part of the understanding.

## Time mechanics

### 9. Your Own Liturgical Year (top 5)
A personal calendar built from your own dates: the day a grandparent died, the day you left home, the week a relationship ended, the morning you got the job. You name the seasons between them, the way a church year has Advent, Lent and Ordinary Time, or a family lights a yahrzeit candle on the anniversary of a death. On each anniversary the app brings back what you wrote last time and asks what you see now, and you add a new layer. After a few years each date holds several readings of the same event, all in your own hand.

**Why it fits:** meaning comes from returning to the same event at different ages, and no one can do that on your behalf.

### 10. Letters Across Time
You write a letter to yourself to be delivered by post in one, five or ten years. When it arrives the rule is that you reply to it, and the reply is sealed for the same interval again. Over decades this becomes a correspondence between versions of you who are free to disagree. You can also write to someone who will outlive you, to be opened at an age you name.

**Why it fits:** the person who answers the letter has lived the years in between, and nobody can stand in for them.

### 11. Birthday Interview
The same twelve questions, asked once a year near your birthday and answered on camera or audio. Before you answer, you watch or hear last year's answers in full. The questions stay fixed for life so that drift shows: what you said you wanted, who you named as important, what scared you. It is closer to the Proust Questionnaire than to a performance review, and there is no score.

**Why it fits:** the evidence is a stack of recordings of you, and you are the only analyst.

### 12. Borrowed Seasons
Once a year you observe one season from a tradition alongside the people who keep it: Lent, Ramadan, the Days of Awe between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, or the Theravada rains retreat (vassa). The app explains what practitioners actually do, what an outsider can respectfully share in (a fast, an iftar invitation, a daily reading) and what belongs to members only, and it suggests contacting a local community first. Afterwards it asks how the weeks went: what was hard, what changed in your days, who you met.

**Why it fits:** a tradition's calendar is lived over weeks with other people, which reading about it can't reproduce.

### 13. The Raft (top 5)
An app designed to withdraw. The name comes from the Buddha's parable of the raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22): a raft is for crossing the river, and you don't carry it on your head once you're across. Each time you report doing something without needing the app (an evening review on your own, a hard conversation held in line with your values), one feature retires, starting with prompts, then reminders, then the daily view. When nothing is left it prints your material as a bound book, exports it as plain files and deletes itself.

**Why it fits:** if the understanding is yours, the tool should become unnecessary, and this one plans for that from the start.

### 14. Sealed Bets
You write short, specific predictions about how you will act. "If my brother asks for money again, I'll say no and still visit him." "By next spring I'll have stopped going to church, or found a reason to stay." Each is sealed with a date, and when the date comes you write what actually happened. The two texts are then shown side by side, without a score.

**Why it fits:** it tests what you believe about yourself against what you later did.

### 15. Sabbath Lock
The app closes for one day a week, chosen by you, and can't be opened on that day. The day is for living what you've been thinking about without recording any of it. It borrows only the shape of a weekly stop; Shabbat itself is much more than an app being closed, and the app says so.

**Why it fits:** some of the work has to happen with no instrument in the room.

### 16. Calendar of Saints
You build a calendar of people whose lives you want to learn from, each with a day: a Desert Mother, Zhuangzi, a nurse from your town, your grandmother. On their day you read something by or about them and do one small thing they would recognise. Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches keep calendars like this, and Auguste Comte made a secular one in 1849. Yours can mix freely, and you can remove someone when you learn something that changes your view of them.

**Why it fits:** you choose your examples and keep revising the list, so the calendar becomes evidence of what you admire.

## Other people as mirrors

### 17. Clearness Committee (top 5)
A kit for running the Quaker clearness committee on a question you're stuck on. You invite four to six people you trust. They may only ask honest, open questions (ones they can't already know the answer to) and may not advise, tell their own stories or steer. There are long silences, you can pass on any question, and everything is confidential, including from you afterwards unless you bring it up. The app supplies the invitation, the ground rules in plain language, a timer and a short guide for the timekeeper, and is put away once the meeting starts.

**Why it fits:** other people help you hear yourself while being forbidden to tell you what you mean.

### 18. Elder Interviews
A guided kit for recording long conversations with grandparents, elders, old neighbours or a retired minister. The questions are about lived belief: what got you through your worst year, what you believed at my age, what you stopped believing, what you'd say to the church or temple if you could. StoryCorps has shown how much people will say when someone simply asks and records. The recording belongs to both people, and the elder decides what is kept.

**Why it fits:** you hear an inherited faith as one specific person lived it, which is often quite different from the doctrine you were taught.

### 19. What They See
You ask five to eight people from different parts of your life to write short answers to three questions: when have you seen me most like myself, what do I seem to care about that I don't say out loud, and when have I seemed out of step with myself. Their answers come back verbatim and unsorted, with no summary and no theme-finding. It draws on the Johari window and on the Reflected Best Self exercise from the University of Michigan.

**Why it fits:** other people hold evidence about you that you can't see from inside, and making sense of it stays your job.

### 20. Standing Circle
Five to seven people commit to meet monthly for one year. At each meeting one person brings an event from their month and speaks, and the others may only reflect back what they heard and ask questions. The shape comes from Quaker worship sharing and from Parker Palmer's Circles of Trust. The app handles scheduling, rotation and the ground rules, and ends the circle after a year unless everyone chooses to continue.

**Why it fits:** a year of being heard by the same people gives your life witnesses, which software can't be.

### 21. Chavruta
Paired study in the Jewish style: two people read one short text slowly, aloud, and argue with it and with each other. The app matches you with a partner (a friend, or by mutual consent a stranger from a different background), supplies a text from any tradition, and stays out of the way for the hour. You don't need to end up agreeing.

**Why it fits:** a partner's objections force you to find out what you actually think about a text.

### 22. Letters Across Traditions
A monthly exchange of paper letters between two people from different traditions, or one with none: a lapsed Catholic and a practising Daoist, a Quaker and a secular Buddhist. Both answer the same question each month from their own life, and neither is trying to persuade. The service handles matching, keeps home addresses private through a forwarding box, and gives either person a clean way to end the exchange.

**Why it fits:** you meet a tradition as one person's lived answer to the question you are also facing.

### 23. The Witness
You tell one person, in a sentence or two, what you are trying to live by this season. Three months later they get a short prompt asking them to tell you honestly what they observed: times you seemed to act on it, times you didn't, and anything they noticed that you never mentioned. Their reply reaches you unedited.

**Why it fits:** someone who watched you supplies evidence about your alignment that you can't produce yourself.

### 24. Question Box
A small wooden box you put up, with permission, in a public place you care about: a café, a library, a launderette. It holds one question on a card and blank slips for anonymous answers. You collect the slips each week and change the question each month, and you can also ask the question face to face of people you'd normally only exchange pleasantries with.

**Why it fits:** strangers widen the range of lived answers you can test your own against, beyond your friends and beyond books.

## Body and place

### 25. Home Pilgrimage
A one-day walk you design from your own door to places that matter: the hospital where you were born, the church you grew up in, a temple you have never entered, a cemetery, a river. You carry a paper credential modelled on the Camino de Santiago credencial and collect a stamp or signature at each stop, ideally from a person there. Each stop has one question printed on the back to answer in pen. The phone stays off except for emergencies.

**Why it fits:** hours of walking between places from your own life give thinking time that can't be shortened.

### 26. Open Doors (top 5)
A guide to attending real gatherings across traditions as a respectful visitor: an unprogrammed Quaker meeting, a Taizé prayer evening, a centering prayer group, a Zen sitting, langar at a gurdwara, Orthodox vespers, a Shabbat service, a mosque open day. For each it covers what happens, what to wear, what to avoid, and what a visitor may and may not join in. It also names honest gaps: in many Western cities the nearest contact with Daoism is a tai chi or qigong class, which is related but different. Afterwards it asks what happened, what you felt and who you spoke to.

**Why it fits:** traditions live in rooms full of people, and how one feels from inside can only be found out by going.

### 27. Samu
Samu is the Zen term for work practice: cleaning, cooking and gardening done with full attention as part of training. Benedictines pair prayer and work in a related way (often summarised as ora et labora, a later motto and not a line from the Rule). You pick one manual task you already do, or one craft you'll learn over a season, and do it in that spirit, noting only what you noticed. The app offers short readings on how each tradition thinks about work and then leaves you to it.

**Why it fits:** understanding that comes through the hands can't be read or summarised into you.

### 28. Return Visits
You go back alone to a place from your past: a childhood street, a school, the flat where something ended. The app gives you three things to do there: stand still for ten minutes, describe what has physically changed, and write what you understand now that you didn't then. Each visit is one dated page with a photo you take.

**Why it fits:** the place has stayed roughly the same while you have changed, and only you can see the difference.

### 29. Cemetery Walk
A slow walk through a local cemetery, reading headstones, once a season. It draws on Buddhist maranasati (mindfulness of death) and on the long Christian and Stoic habit of keeping mortality in view. The questions are few: which inscriptions stopped you, what would you want said about you, and what would have to change for that to be true.

**Why it fits:** considering your own death changes what seems important, and that only happens when you are the one standing there.

### 30. Felt Sense First
Before you write anything about what an experience meant, you describe where it sits in your body and what it feels like, in plain physical words. This comes from Eugene Gendlin's Focusing, and it echoes the Ignatian attention to inner movements of consolation and desolation. Only after the physical description can you add an interpretation, and the two are kept side by side.

**Why it fits:** your body's response is data only you have access to, and it often disagrees with the first story you tell.

### 31. Guesthouse Stay
Many monasteries and retreat centres take guests: Benedictine houses (the Rule tells monks to receive every guest as Christ), Zen centres, Plum Village practice centres, some ashrams. The app lists ways to stay for three to seven days, with costs, rules and what guests are expected to do, and suggests bringing one question only. You keep the house's timetable and talk to whoever is willing.

**Why it fits:** a week inside a tradition's daily timetable teaches things that reading about it can't.

## Contribution and society

### 32. Season of Repair
Adapted from teshuvah in the Days of Awe. Jewish tradition (Mishnah Yoma 8:9) holds that Yom Kippur doesn't atone for wrongs between people until the person wronged has been reconciled. Once a year you name one person you owe an apology or a repair to, work out what making it right would actually involve, and do it. You record what you did and how they responded.

**Why it fits:** the outcome depends on another person's response, which sits outside your head and outside your control.

### 33. Tithe of Hours
A tithe taken in time: a fixed share of your free hours given regularly to people or work beyond yourself. Christian tithing, Sikh dasvandh (a tenth of earnings) and Islamic zakat are the precedents, and the app is clear that it borrows the idea of a fixed share and none of their religious obligations. You choose the share and the place. The app records hours given and now and then asks what you learned from the people you were with.

**Why it fits:** your values meet real needs and real people, which rarely match what you planned.

### 34. The Long Table
Once a season you host a simple meal for people you don't usually eat with: a neighbour, a colleague's family, someone new to the area. One question goes on the table and anyone may answer or pass. The shape echoes langar, the Shabbat table, iftar and the early Christian agape meal, without taking their names or rites.

**Why it fits:** hospitality turns a private philosophy into something other people experience and respond to.

### 35. Hidden Gift
Maimonides set out eight levels of giving (tzedakah), and near the top is giving where neither giver nor receiver knows the other. Once a month you do one thing for someone anonymously and tell nobody. The only record is a private note on whether you wanted credit and what that wanting was like.

**Why it fits:** it separates acting on a value from being seen to act on it, a difference only you can observe.

### 36. Ask the Affected
After an outward act that came out of what you are working through, such as a change in how you run your team or more time with a parent, you ask the person on the other end one short question: did it make any difference to you? Their answer is stored next to what you intended. Over time you can see the gap, if there is one, between what you meant and what landed.

**Why it fits:** the world's response is evidence that introspection can't supply.

### 37. Ethical Will (top 5)
An ethical will (tzava'ah in Hebrew) is a Jewish practice with medieval examples: a letter that leaves values, lessons and requests to the people who come after you, rather than possessions. You write one each year to named people (your children, a niece, a friend) and keep every version. You decide whether and when any of them is delivered, now or after your death.

**Why it fits:** writing to people you love forces you to be specific, and the yearly versions show how your values actually shifted.

## Where AI fits

The principles say that when software is tempted to conclude something, it asks a question instead. These are concrete roles that keep to that rule, followed by uses to avoid.

### 38. Questions Only
An AI mode whose output is checked before it is shown: it must be one or more questions, with no "you should", no "this means" and no claims about your feelings. If you ask what an event means, it says plainly that it won't answer that, gives the reason once, and offers a question, a tradition's lens or a person you might ask instead.

**Why it fits:** the model's fluency goes into sharper questions, and the answer stays unwritten until you write it.

### 39. Concordance
A concordance is an index of every place a word appears in a text; Bible students have used them for centuries. Here the text is your own writing. You can ask where you have used "trapped", "home" or your father's name, and it lists dated quotes with links and counts over time. It may point out a recurring word on its own only after several months of material, always quoting at least three entries, and you mark each pattern yes, no or not yet.

**Why it fits:** it shows your own words back with citations and leaves the reading of them to you.

### 40. Labelled Voice
An AI that can speak in the manner of a specific strand or text, such as the Stoicism of Epictetus's Discourses or the Daodejing in a named translation, always visibly labelled as a simulation. Every reply cites the passages it draws on and marks where it is paraphrasing or extending. It declines to voice living Indigenous traditions, living teachers or God. This is also the standard any tradition voice in Council should meet.

**Why it fits:** you can argue with a tradition's logic while always knowing where the real source ends.

### 41. Second Hearing
For the tradition you were raised in and drifted from, the AI shows you the strands you probably never met and asks which version you actually rejected. For someone raised in a church that never struck a chord, that might be the Desert Mothers and Fathers, Meister Eckhart, Simone Weil, Quaker silence or liberation theology, each in its own words with sources. For each one it asks whether your objection applies to that strand as well, and it makes no attempt to argue you back in.

**Why it fits:** it widens what you are choosing between and leaves the choosing with you.

### 42. Librarian
It points you to primary texts and good translations by strand, with honest notes ("this translation is loose", "this popular quote is misattributed"). It will not summarise a text in place of your reading it. After you read, it asks what stopped you and where you disagreed, and keeps your answers.

**Why it fits:** reading the source yourself is part of the work, and a summary skips that part.

### 43. Rehearsal Partner
It helps you prepare for human conversations: drafting open questions before sitting on a friend's clearness committee, planning an elder interview, working out how to begin an apology. It plays the other person only if you ask, and it steps aside before the real conversation.

**Why it fits:** it makes the human encounter more likely to happen and go well, without taking its place.

### 44. Slow Echo
Thirty days after you write something, it quotes a line back to you exactly and asks one question: do you still think this? Your answer is stored next to the original.

**Why it fits:** it brings time into your thinking without adding any opinion of its own.

### Anti-patterns to avoid

- Interpreting events, dreams or coincidences ("this suggests you're ready for change").
- Summarising your year, your entries or "what you've learned", because the summary would replace the memory.
- Typing the user or matching them to a tradition with a score ("72% Daoist").
- Writing your creed, prayer, letter or journal entry, including a "help me phrase it" that ends with its words standing in for yours.
- Inventing, merging or tidying quotes from scripture or teachers.
- Simulating God, a dead relative or a living teacher.
- Acting as an always-available companion that replaces friends, a community or a therapist.
- Optimising for engagement with re-entry notifications, streaks or personalised hooks.
- Mood scoring or sentiment tracking presented as insight.
- Reassurance on demand, such as telling you that you are on the right path.

## Wild cards

### 45. Anti-Horoscope
A weekly column with the form and tone of a horoscope, where every line comes from your own commitments and earlier words. "This week you are someone who calls your sister. On Thursday, the you of last March said you would stop agreeing to things you resent." It arrives on a fixed day like a newspaper column. It keeps what draws people to astrology, a voice from outside telling you who you are this week, and fills it with your own material.

**Why it fits:** the oracle can only repeat what you have already said to yourself.

### 46. Proxy Week
You and another person swap written rules for living (such as the Rule of Life from The Long Way or a Working Creed) and each try to live by the other's for a week. At the end each reports what was unclear, impossible, surprisingly good or simply wrong for them. Monastic rules like Benedict's were written to be lived by other people, and this tests whether yours can be.

**Why it fits:** watching someone else try to live your words shows you what they actually ask of a person.

### 47. Opposite Week
You spend a week living by the tradition you feel least drawn to, using sources written by its practitioners: its schedule, its practices, its ethics as they describe them. You note what you resisted and what surprised you. The principles count pushback as data, and this produces a lot of it.

**Why it fits:** finding out exactly what you reject, and why, is part of finding out what you hold.

### 48. Nothing Kept
A mode in which nothing is saved. You write, speak or draw, and when you finish it is deleted; if you used paper, the app suggests burning it. It counters the assumption that every reflection should be archived and mined for patterns. Some people work through grief or anger more honestly when they know nothing will be read later.

**Why it fits:** the processing happens in you, and keeping no record shows it never depended on one.

### 49. Living Eulogy
Friends gather while you're alive and each says what they would say at your funeral, then what they hope they'll be able to say by then. Some organisations in South Korea have run "living funerals" as a reflective exercise; this version is smaller and personal. You only listen, and afterwards you write down what you heard.

**Why it fits:** hearing your life described by people who have watched it is a mirror you can't hold up yourself.

## Risks and failure modes

- **Rumination dressed as reflection** (going over the same hurt with no movement). *Mitigation:* limit how often one event can be reopened, space returns by weeks, and end most prompts with an act or a question for another person.
- **Spiritual bypassing** (John Welwood's term for using spiritual ideas to skip pain, anger or responsibility). *Mitigation:* pair inner practices with repair and asking the affected, and ask what you did as often as what you realised.
- **Cultural appropriation and flattening.** *Mitigation:* use practitioners' own sources and named strands, state clearly what outsiders may join, leave closed practices closed, and include no Indigenous ceremony content.
- **Tradition shopping** (keeping the comforting parts and dropping the demanding ones). *Mitigation:* show each tradition's hard demands next to its comforts, and ask which parts you left out and why.
- **Dependence or cult dynamics.** *Mitigation:* no founder voice or central teacher, planned withdrawal as in The Raft, rotating facilitation, and circles that end after a year by default.
- **Family strain** (questioning the faith you were raised in can hurt relationships). *Mitigation:* approach the home tradition with curiosity through Elder Interviews and Second Hearing, and never nudge the user to announce conclusions.
- **Privacy, including other people's words.** *Mitigation:* local storage, contributions from others held under their consent and deletable by them, elders controlling their recordings, and nothing sent to an AI model without a per-item choice (or on-device models only).
- **The guilt machine.** *Mitigation:* no streaks, no counts of missed days, paths that wait, and ambient pieces like The Bell and the Almanac Frame that ask nothing of you.
- **Invented or distorted sources.** *Mitigation:* AI may only quote verbatim from a curated, cited library, and any paraphrase is labelled as one.
- **Crisis moments** (acute grief, self-harm, abuse). *Mitigation:* clear routes to human help shown in plain words, and no reflective prompt offered in place of that help.
- **Safety with strangers** (pen pals, swaps, question boxes). *Mitigation:* opt-in only, forwarding addresses, no personal details exchanged by default, and an easy way to end contact.
- **Cost and access** (pilgrimages, retreats and devices cost money). *Mitigation:* every practice has a free, local, paper version.
- **Performance** (reflecting for an audience). *Mitigation:* no feed, no public profiles, and nothing shareable by default.
- **A hidden worldview** (the app's own individualist assumption that a person picks a tradition for themselves). *Mitigation:* name that assumption openly, represent traditions in which faith is received and communal on their own terms, and have practitioners review the content.
